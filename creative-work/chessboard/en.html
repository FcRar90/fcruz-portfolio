Chessboard

A coffee table centerpiece as much as a chess set. I wanted a board with a strong geometric character, so I gave each piece its own cut, distinct enough to tell the two sides apart at a glance, and shaped so the whole set folds down into its own storage compartments once the game is over.

A piece meant as much for playing on as for looking at.
01 — The Process

The board started as raw stock, cut down into individual checker squares on a table saw, then glued up square by square into the playing surface itself. The pieces came next, each one shaped and cut by hand until every piece had its own silhouette, something you could tell apart by touch as much as by sight.

Squares cut and laid out on the bench before glue-up.
A section of the board clamped while the glue sets.

02 — The Material

The board is built from two woods: Conacaste and Palo Blanco. Conacaste is soft, with prominent veins and a rich, dark hue, and Palo Blanco is harder, lighter, with a more subtle grain. Put next to each other, they give the board and the pieces a natural contrast between the two sides, no stain or dye needed.

Conacaste and Palo Blanco pieces, set up on the board.
The geometric cut of each piece, up close.
03 — Storage and Finish

The board doubles as its own case. Underneath the playing surface is a fabric-lined drawer where every piece has its spot, so the set that was just out on the table a minute ago packs away into something that looks just as much at home closed. The finish is a few rounds of fine sanding and a light coat of clear matte varnish, enough to protect the wood from dust and water without hiding the grain underneath.
